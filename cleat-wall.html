The cleat wall

Your tools are on the floor, on the bench, in a bin you dig through every time you need the drill. A French cleat wall fixes that, but it looks like a weekend of math you'd rather not get wrong.

Guess, and you're back at the store for the sheet you were one strip short on, or you screw a rail into drywall and watch a loaded shelf pry itself off the wall.

Type your wall and you get the cut list: how many rails and at what heights, how many 45° rips, how many sheets, where every stud and screw lands. Here's a 10-foot wall behind a workbench under an 8-foot ceiling: eleven rails, eight 45° rips, one sheet for the rails, 176 screws, every one in a stud.

Nothing you type here leaves your device. No account, no server, no analytics. Your numbers live in this browser tab and are erased when you close it. "Start over" wipes them now.

Why this one exists

About 90% of my garage hangs on a French cleat. It's the reason I never had to buy a shed. Three years ago I turned an office nook into one; this fall that same wall became the backpack hub. My first hangers kept falling off, worst with shelf brackets, until I added a stop. And when I asked an AI to draw me a cleat, it drew it backwards and forgot the other half. Once you get the 45 down, you can do a whole garage in a weekend. This does the math for that weekend.

The stop

A block on the bottom of each hanger, as thick as the cleat, bears on the wall below so weight pushes in instead of prying out.

Six at the bench, twelve everywhere else

I'm a physical therapist, and I spaced my wall like a workstation. Hand tools go in front of the bench, waist high and up, inside a normal reach, on rails 6 inches apart. Everything else gets 12. The top rail is above easy reach on purpose: long things hang from it and you grab them low.
