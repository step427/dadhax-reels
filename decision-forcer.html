Triage protocol · two questions · one deadline

The decision forcer

Every other decision tool on the internet asks you which option. This one doesn't care which option. It asks whether this decision deserves any more of your life — and sets the date it has to die.

one decision · two questions · a deadline that leaves this page

Nothing you write here leaves your device. No account, no server, no analytics — what you type is held in this browser tab so you can reload without losing it, and erased when you close the tab. "Start over" wipes it now. I can't read it. Nobody can.

Why this one exists

I went looking for a tool that does this and there isn't one. Every decision app on the market is either a randomizer — spin a wheel, flip a coin — or a weighted matrix where you score options in a grid. All of them answer "which one?"

Not one of them asks the question that actually costs you: does this decision deserve more time, and when does it have to be over? The one-way/two-way door idea is all over the internet as something to read. Nowhere as something you can use. So this is the instrument version.

It sits upstream of every coin-flipper out there. You don't need help picking. You need permission to stop circling.

It triages, it doesn't compare

Most decisions aren't hard. They're just undecided. This sorts the ones worth agonizing over from the ones you should have called last Tuesday.

It ends with a date, not a feeling

A verdict you nod at changes nothing. This one hands you a calendar line and a message to one named person — because a deadline nobody else knows about is just a wish.

It costs you two taps

Two questions. That's the whole instrument. Anything longer and you'd be using the tool to avoid the decision — which is the exact thing it's for.

Part 1 — Name it out loud

Write the decision as a sentence someone else could read. Vague in, vague out — "the job thing" is not a decision, it's a mood.

Part 2 — Which door is it?

If this goes badly, can you walk back through? Not "would it be awkward" — can it actually be undone.

Where this comes fromJeff Bezos, 2016 Amazon shareholder letter — the one-way / two-way door distinction. Most people treat every decision like a one-way door. Most decisions aren't.

Part 3 — Do you have enough?

Not all of it. Enough. There's no slider here on purpose — a slider is one more decision, and you came here with too many already.

The 70% rule — same 2016 letterMost decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70 percent of the information you wish you had. If you wait for 90 percent, in most cases, you're probably being slow.

Part 4 — What it's already cost you

Optional, and the verdict works without it. But this is the part people don't look at: the decision has been charging you rent this whole time.
