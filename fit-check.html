The fit check

Your basement sketch looks right on paper. Then your studs go up, the inspector walks in, and your toilet is two inches too close to the wall. Now you move the wall, or the drain, in concrete. The first stud is where a bad plan gets expensive.

Type the sizes off your sketch and each one is checked against the common code minimums: inches to spare, or inches short. My plan puts my toilet 15½ inches from the shower glass. That clears 15 by half an inch.

Nothing you type here leaves your device. No account, no server, no analytics. Your numbers are erased when you close the tab.

Why this one exists

Nothing in my basement is framed yet. I walked it with a laser and read every wall out loud to Claude. The long wall came back 591, then 583, then 584⅝, so I averaged it. Then I stood where the bathroom goes and said it on camera: "I don't know what's standard here." Then: "three feet, what's a normal shower?" Most of us don't know, until an inspector tells us. The AI version, in the shop: Instagram, Facebook.

The fit

Bring these to the building department before you buy a stud:

    this page isn't your inspector These are common minimums from the model residential code most U.S. towns start from. Your town adopts its own edition and amends it, and a finished basement usually needs a permit. Their number wins. Call before you frame. Not checked here: smoke and CO alarms, the window well and its ladder, a walled-in furnace or water heater, electrical and gas.
    The boulder question

    Most problems are who-you-know. Name one person in your town who has finished a basement.

    Now make the call — ask: “did you pull a permit, and what did the inspector flag?”
