One flip · start to finish

One old house

Thinking about your first flip? This is mine, in order. Eight short chapters with the numbers left in. Each one ends with what it cost me, so it can cost you less.

The short version. A 1908 house, bought in the 130s. Everyone said too far gone. The work ran about six months late, the buyer walked, and I still own it. One flip is my whole résumé as an investor.

01

It started with $3,000

2008. $3,000 down on a $40,000 condo. Five years and about $7,000 of remodel later it sold for $100,000. That rolled into the next house, and that one into the one I live in now. All three were homes. This log is about the fourth, the first I bought as an investment.

Keep this Getting in cost $3,000. Getting started as an investor took 17 more years. You can shorten the second number.

02

Day one

Two days before closing I walked the house with my phone out. Out front: "Let's see what we can do here." On the attic stairs: "Don't think we'll have room in the budget to touch." In the basement, under a rusted pipe: "Okay, that's not good."

By January the attic had heat, drywall and paint. By March it had carpet.

Keep this I said the attic wasn't in the budget, then finished the attic. Decide what you won't touch before you close, and write it down.

03

Later than anybody told me

The day I closed, I texted the contractor: "The goal's under 3 months or I start bleeding cash." We settled on three. By the end of January the work was past that and not finished. All told it ran about six months late, and by my math that cost me close to five figures in carrying costs and interest.

My notes from January say "honestly, my fault." I had no detailed contract. Every investor I asked afterward said the same thing: the investor brings the contract, with deductions for late work written in.

Keep this Bring your own contract, with a finish date and a deduction for late work. My template cost me close to five figures and about six months.

04

"Look what I did"

Twenty-nine seconds. I open a set of doors, stand in the doorway with my arms out and say, "Look what I did. You'd be surprised how hard that was in this old house." I tagged it #tired.

Keep this In a house this old, small jobs take longer than they look. Budget their hours like big ones.

05

Bookshelves or doors

April, almost eight months in. I slide a pair of doors open and shut and ask, "Bookshelves or doors?" I get as far as "Imagine they'd be open most of the time, but" and say good night. That is the entire video.

Keep this Late in a job you stop being able to see it. Ask someone who hasn't stared at it for eight months.

06

"I shouldn't be swinging a hammer." Yet.

I built the mailbox sign myself with a coaching call running. When it was done I turned the camera on and said, "I shouldn't be swinging a hammer. Still waiting for that day." Then: "Yet."

Keep this The owner's hours feel free and they aren't. Price your own hour before you pick up the tool.

07

I hope a family gets it

In January I said on camera that I was prepared to rent the place out instead of selling it. In April, rained out and sitting in the truck, I said I hoped a family just starting out would get it. It listed and went under contract. Then the buyer walked.

Keep this The exit you plan is one option. Have the second one ready before you list. I'd said mine out loud in January, and it's the one I used.

08

Done, and what it actually cost

The numbers, in ranges: bought in the 130s, all in the mid-260s, appraised at 290. On paper that is about $25,000, and none of it arrived as a check.

With the buyer gone I refinanced and kept the house. It's leased now. The refinance was supposed to take two weeks and took six. One of those weeks was mine: I let an email sit over the Fourth of July. I was learning AI while all this was happening, so I built a system that tracks every open thread. It closed with two days to spare.

Keep this My profit is still inside the house. Plan for the version where yours is too.

2026-10-07 Crooked wall? Scribe the filler. (10 numbered steps)

The wall next to the cabinet isn't straight, so a straight filler strip leaves a gap. Here's how I scribed one to fit.

1. Clamp the filler blank on the cabinet face: edge flush with the cabinet's outside, bottom at final height. 2. Hold a scrap stick in the gap, end on the wall, and mark the cabinet. That's a story stick. No numbers to read, so you can't misread an eighth. 3. Transfer that gap onto the blank, measured from the edge that will sit against the cabinet. 4. Put the stick's end back on the wall at the same height and mark where it meets that line. That's your scribe. 5. Drill a pencil hole through the stick on the scribe mark. Brace it on the bench. 6. Ride the stick's end down the wall, square to it. The pencil copies the wall onto the blank. 7. Check it. The gap was 1-5/8. The blank at the line read 1-5/8. 8. Rip on the table saw just past the widest point of the line. Narrow rip, so use a push stick. Then jigsaw on the waste side. Save the line. 9. Sand right up to the line. I back-beveled the edge a little, taking it off the back, so only the front edge has to touch the wall. 10. Test fit, then nail it home. Keep your free hand a foot from the nail. Brads can curl out.

Hairline left over? Caulk and paint makes it what it ain't.

Save this for your next trim job so you don't lose an hour scrolling to find it again. Send it to a DIYer who needs it. Better yet, teach it to one. If you can teach it, you've got it.

2026-10-07 Wood filler you can't buy

Watch it on Instagram →
This one only exists there — Instagram stopped letting sites embed reels.

Wood filler you can't buy in a store: wood glue plus the sawdust off the project you're filling.

1. Save the dust. 2. Blender for extra fine dust. 3. Baker's sifter so only the finest dust gets through. That's what fills the smallest gaps. 4. Glue in the gap, dust on top. Pat, push, rub it in. 5. Cardboard placemat underneath so you can pour the extra back. 6. Let it dry, then sand it flush.

The color matches because it IS the board. Every cut, the blade turns about an eighth of an inch into dust. That eighth is your filler.

Save this for the next gap you fill.
